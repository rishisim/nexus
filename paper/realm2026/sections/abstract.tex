Iterative language-model agents spend additional inference in the hope of
recovering errors made by simpler readers. We test a necessary precondition for
that trade-off: whether the expensive expert is actually complementary. In a
prespecified, controlled-evidence development study spanning five financial-QA
datasets, bounded ReAct was less accurate and more costly than structured
one-call reasoning. On the official scores, only 10 of 200 routing examples
were ReAct-only successes, versus 42 Static-only successes; the fallback gate
then escalated 70\% of cases and reduced exact accuracy from 0.395 to 0.295. A
prospectively frozen 75-item replication with a second model family repeated the
configured-system ordering: Static scored 0.309 versus ReAct's 0.068, with 19
versus one unique exact success. A deterministic trace audit nevertheless
flagged 28/52 original one-sided cases as scorer-sensitive candidates because a
rejected output visibly contained the reference value or a close form; the
methods also used different answer contracts. We retain the official scores and
do not treat these flags as human adjudication. We honored the locked stop and
did not execute the 900-example final partition. Agent-routing studies should
audit expert complementarity together with prompt and scoring contracts before
optimizing when to escalate.
